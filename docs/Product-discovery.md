# Product Discovery

## 1. The 60-Second Problem Pitch

### The Problem

Standardized, routine government applications take weeks or months to process because manual case-officer reviews create massive administrative backlogs.

Causes:

- Demographic change -> not enough employees
- Unsexy industry i.e., repetitive work -> not enough employees
- In Germany -  civil cervants  -> expensive and hard to lay off

As a result:

- Simple services take long
- Short opening hours

### The Customer

- Newly arrived international professionals and expats in Germany navigating municipal registration and residency administration.
- Residents
- Government

### Why You

Personal experience with bureaucratic friction in public administration and a drive to build seamless infrastructure for both citizens and municipal caseworkers.

### Problem Statement

We believe citizens (in Germany) struggle to complete administrative work. Today they wait weeks for limited in-person appointments or submit manual paper/PDF applications, which costs the government money and limits innovation.

## 2. Facts and Figures

- Whole services are missing. A German Economic Institute study found that of 575 services due online by end of 2022, only 166 were available everywhere in the country.
- Online forms sit on top of old processes. A study from the bidt institute notes that the services that are online largely still conceal analogue processes, and no instrument yet obliges local authorities to use standardised solutions.
- The benchmark shows it's possible. Estonia has 99% of public services online and follows a "once-only" principle: the state cannot ask citizens for the same information twice.

## 5. Interviews

### Customer interviews: digital Bürgeramt

These five interviews are synthetic, written to rehearse the pitch and sharpen hypotheses. They are not evidence: replace them with real conversations before presenting any of this as findings.

### The five interviews

The first two are newcomers, your core customer. The third is a long-term resident, the fourth a caseworker and the fifth the municipal buyer.

#### 5.1 Priya, 29, software engineer from India, Berlin (newcomer)

- **Situation:** Arrived in February on an EU Blue Card. The first available Bürgeramt slot for her Anmeldung was nine weeks out.
- **What hurt:** Without the registration certificate she could not open a regular bank account, and her landlord was slow to sign the Wohnungsgeberbestätigung. Every other task waited on that one piece of paper.
- **Workaround:** She refreshed the booking page daily and finally got a cancellation slot through a Telegram group of expats. Her employer also paid a relocation agency about €1,200 for help.
- **eID:** Never activated. The PIN letter got lost and she did not know what the card could do.
- **Quote:** "The appointment itself took twelve minutes. Getting it took nine weeks."
- **Takeaway:** The pain is the queue and the knock-on blockage, not the form. Her employer already pays to avoid it, which suggests a second payer.

#### 5.2 Mateo, 24, master's student from Colombia, Munich (newcomer)

- **Situation:** Needed to extend his student residence permit while working a part-time job that depends on a valid status.
- **What hurt:** His email to the Ausländerbehörde went unanswered for six weeks. He had no idea whether it had been read, which stage his case was at, or whether he could keep working.
- **Language:** Forms were German only. Machine translation turned one field into something he was afraid to answer.
- **eID:** Someone mentioned his residence permit card has an online ID function. Nobody had ever shown him how to switch it on.
- **Quote:** "I don't need it fast. I need to know where it is."
- **Takeaway:** Status visibility and plain-language guidance matter as much as raw speed, and for him more.

#### 5.3 Anna, 38, teacher, Hamburg (long-term resident)

- **Situation:** Born and raised in Germany. Her change of address went through online in about twenty minutes and she has no complaint about it.
- **What hurt:** Parental allowance after her second child. She entered the same address, employer and birth date into forms at three different offices.
- **Trust:** She would not give her ID data to a new app from an unknown company. She trusts "anything with .de" and is tired of yet another login.
- **Quote:** "The state already knows my address, my employer and my child's birth date. Why am I typing it again?"
- **Takeaway:** This is your first disconfirming signal. For people who already know the system, the Anmeldung is not the pain. The once-only problem is, and a startup brand has to earn trust that a government site gets for free.

#### 5.4 Frank, 52, caseworker in a Bürgeramt of a mid-sized city

- **Situation:** Has worked at the counter for 21 years. Four positions in his team have been open for two years.
- **What hurt:** By his estimate, about one in three appointments ends with a missing document or the wrong form, so the person has to come back and take another slot. Roughly one in ten slots is a no-show.
- **Tools:** Everything runs through the city's legacy case software. He has no admin rights and no say in what gets bought. Any new tool that adds clicks to his day will be ignored.
- **Quote:** "Nobody here is afraid of being replaced. We're afraid of being buried."
- **Takeaway:** He would welcome pre-checked, complete applications. The pitch to caseworkers is relief from backlog, not automation of their job, and the product must plug into the existing case software.

#### 5.5 Dr. Katrin Möller, 47, head of digitalisation, city of about 180,000 (the buyer)

- **Situation:** Decides which digital services the city offers. Her budget comes mostly from project funding, and any larger purchase takes nine to fourteen months to procure.
- **What hurt:** Many pilots, few rollouts. She cannot buy anything that duplicates a federal or state service, so she asks first whether it can reuse one that already exists.
- **Requirements:** Hosting in Germany or the EU, GDPR compliance, and an interface to her case-software vendor. A product that locks her in is a non-starter.
- **What she would buy on:** Measurable results. Counter hours freed, share of complete applications and days to decision.
- **Quote:** "Show me a Bürgeramt that gave back a fifth of its counter hours and I'll find the budget."
- **Takeaway:** The buyer is the municipality and the proof point is a reference city. A complement to existing state infrastructure sells better than a rival to it.

### What the five add up to

Four of five confirm the pain you described, and the one that does not (Anna) narrows your starting customer to the newcomer.

| Interviewee | Role | Biggest pain | Who pays or decides | Supports your hypothesis |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Priya | Newcomer | Nine-week wait, blocked bank account | Employer paid about €1,200 for an agency | Yes |
| Mateo | Newcomer | No case status, German-only forms | Nobody, student budget | Partly: wants transparency more than speed |
| Anna | Resident | Re-typing the same data at several offices | Nobody | Weak for Anmeldung, strong for once-only |
| Frank | Caseworker | Incomplete applications, no-shows, backlog | Has no purchase say | Yes, if it fits his case software |
| Möller | Municipal buyer | Pilots that never roll out, slow procurement | She decides, via project funding | Yes, if the results are measurable |

### What this changes in your idea

- Start with the Anmeldung for newcomers. It is the step that blocks everything after it, and it is where the two newcomers felt the most pain.
- Sell to municipalities, test employers as a second payer. Priya's employer already pays an agency to get around the queue.
- Add status tracking and plain-language, multilingual guidance. Mateo's need is cheap to build and easy to demo.
- Position as a pre-checking layer in front of the existing case software. Frank's and Möller's objections both come down to integration and lock-in.
- Reuse the official ID login instead of asking for a new account. Anna's trust gap is the main risk for a private brand.

## 6. Using this in your 60-second pitch

Open with one question to a live audience member, then branch on the answer. The Sam question works because it puts your own hypothesis at risk in front of the room.

1. Ask: "Sam, you moved to Portugal. What was your biggest headache with bureaucracy?"
2. If the answer is about waiting, appointments or one document blocking another: "That is exactly why we are building this." Then give one real quote from a newcomer (Priya's nine-week wait is the shape to look for) and the Anmeldung chain that blocks a bank account and a residence permit.
3. If the answer is something else: thank Sam and move on without defending the idea. Tie it to the call where you found out seven minutes into an hour that the problem was not your client's: a fast no saves everyone's time. Anna's interview is your proof that a no sharpened the focus to newcomers.
4. Close on the buyer: the city gets counter hours back and fewer incomplete applications, which is what Möller said she would pay for.

Use only real quotes in the final pitch. The ones in this doc are placeholders for the shape of what you should be listening for.

## 7. Before the real interviews

Four hypotheses from the simulated set need real evidence, and each has a question that asks about past behaviour, not opinions.

| Hypothesis to test | Question to ask | Who to ask |
|---|---|---|
| Newcomers wait weeks for the Anmeldung and it blocks other tasks | "Walk me through the last time you registered. What could you not do until it was done?" | Newcomers who arrived in the last 12 months |
| Someone already pays to avoid the queue | "What did you try while waiting? Did anyone pay for help?" | Newcomers, HR and relocation staff |
| Caseworkers would adopt pre-checked applications | "What share of appointments ends with something missing, and what happens next?" | Bürgeramt caseworkers |
| Municipalities can buy outside one-off pilot funding | "How was your last digital service bought, how long did it take and who paid?" | Municipal digitalisation officers |

Aim for at least three newcomers, one caseworker and one municipal contact. University international offices, expat groups and your own network are the quickest routes in. Keep one eye out for a real Anna: a person for whom the problem does not exist is as valuable as the ones who confirm it.
